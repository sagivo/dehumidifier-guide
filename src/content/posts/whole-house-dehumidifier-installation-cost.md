The unit price is the number everyone shops, but installation is where whole-house dehumidifier budgets are actually won or lost. Two neighbors can buy the same $1,800 dehumidifier and pay $2,200 and $4,500 installed — the difference is entirely ductwork, drainage, electrical, and where the unit lives. Here's what drives the number and how to keep it honest.

## The national numbers

HomeGuide's 2026 cost data puts the national average at **$2,200 installed**, with a typical range of **$1,500–$3,500**, a $1,200 low end, and a $4,000 high end for standard jobs. BobVila's 2026 reporting lands in the same band at $1,300–$2,800 including the unit.

The anatomy of the bill:

| Component | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Dehumidifier unit (70–80 pints) | $1,100–$1,700 |
| Dehumidifier unit (90–100 pints) | $1,300–$2,000 |
| Dehumidifier unit (120–200 pints) | $1,500–$2,300 |
| Labor — straightforward tie-in | $500–$1,000 |
| Labor — complex (attic/crawl/dedicated return) | $900–$1,500 |
| Condensate pump + misc parts | $150–$500 |
| Extra ductwork (if needed) | $1,000–$2,700 |

Sources: HomeGuide and contractor-reported ranges, 2026. Treat every figure as a moving target — confirm with local quotes.

## What the installer actually does

A ducted install isn't "plug it in." The standard **dedicated return to supply** method — the one most contractors recommend — works like this:

1. A dedicated return vent is cut in, feeding house air into the dehumidifier
2. The dehumidifier dries that air and discharges it into the HVAC supply plenum
3. The unit runs independently of the air conditioner, so it dehumidifies even when the AC isn't calling
4. A condensate drain line is run to a floor drain, sump, or pump
5. The humidistat/control is wired — either the unit's onboard control or a wall-mounted living-space control

The skilled parts are cutting and sealing ductwork without wrecking the furnace's static pressure, the low-voltage control wiring, and routing drainage that won't freeze, kink, or back up. That's what the labor line buys, and it's why the [how-to-choose guide](/posts/how-to-choose-whole-house-dehumidifier/) steers most buyers toward a pro for ducted setups.

## Three real-world budget scenarios

**Scenario A — Easy tie-in, gravity drain: $1,800–$2,700.**
Unit $1,200–$1,800, labor $600–$900. The classic case: utility room or basement next to the air handler, existing ductwork cooperates, floor drain nearby. An [AprilAire E070 Pro](/dehumidifiers/aprilaire-e070-pro/) in a basement with a floor drain is the textbook Scenario A.

**Scenario B — Crawl space or attic, pump required: $2,450–$4,100.**
Unit $1,500–$2,300, labor $800–$1,400, pump and parts $150–$400. Tight access, awkward drain routing, and a condensate pump move the job into this band. A [pump-equipped unit like the AlorAir HDi90](/dehumidifiers/alorair-sentinel-hdi90/) can simplify Scenario B by eliminating the separate pump.

**Scenario C — New dedicated return / ventilation intake: $3,500–$6,700.**
Unit $1,600–$2,500, labor $900–$1,500, ductwork and parts $1,000–$2,700. Less common, but real when the HVAC layout won't cooperate or fresh-air ventilation is part of the plan. If your quote lands here, make sure you understand exactly what ductwork is being added and why.

If a quote falls far outside these bands in either direction, check the scope before signing — or before celebrating. A suspiciously cheap quote often omits the drain plan, the electrical work, or the permit.

## DIY vs pro: an honest accounting

**DIY saves $500–$2,000 in labor.** The skills required, per contractor guidance: HVAC system knowledge, electrical wiring (possibly 240V), ductwork fabrication and modification, plumbing for drainage, and building-code familiarity. Budget 8–16 hours.

**The catches:** manufacturer warranties sometimes require professional installation; code compliance becomes your problem; incorrect ductwork reduces efficiency and shortens the unit's life; and refrigerant/electrical work carries real safety risk. Unless you're genuinely experienced with HVAC, this is a hire-it-out job — the risk you're paying the contractor to absorb is real.

**The middle path:** buy the unit yourself (often cheaper than contractor markup), handle the freestanding placement and drain hose yourself if applicable, and hire the pro only for the duct tie-in and electrical. Many contractors will quote labor-only.

## How to get a quote you can trust

1. **Get three quotes.** The spread tells you as much as any single number.
2. **Demand itemization:** unit model number, labor hours, ductwork scope, electrical scope, pump make/model, permit fees. Vague quotes hide vague work.
3. **Ask about the drain plan specifically.** "Where does the water go?" is the question that separates careful contractors from hand-wavers.
4. **Confirm the model number, not just the brand.** "An AprilAire" could be the $1,550 E070 or the $2,600 E130 — a 70% swing hiding in one word.
5. **Ask about ventilation.** Some contractors bundle fresh-air intake; know whether yours does and whether you want it.

## What's usually NOT in the quote

Quotes vary most in what they silently exclude. When comparing, confirm each of these is either included or explicitly yours to handle:

- **Electrical panel work.** If the panel is full or the run is long, a sub-panel or extended circuit adds $200–600. Most quotes assume a straightforward circuit.
- **Permits.** Some jurisdictions require a mechanical permit for HVAC-tied installs ($50–200). Ask who pulls it.
- **Condensate pump.** Frequently quoted as an adder or omitted entirely — confirm. See our [pump planning guide](/posts/crawl-space-dehumidifier-with-pump/).
- **Old unit removal.** Replacing an existing dehumidifier? Disposal is sometimes a separate line.
- **Ductwork modifications beyond the tie-in.** The quote usually covers the dehumidifier's own connections, not fixing your existing ductwork's problems. If the contractor discovers crushed or disconnected ducts mid-job, that's a change order.
- **Controls and wiring to the living space.** A wall-mounted humidistat in the living area (vs. the unit's onboard control) may be an adder — worth it for crawl space and attic installs where you can't see the unit's display.

The pattern: the cheaper the quote, the more of these it likely omits. An itemized $3,000 quote is usually better value than a lump-sum $2,200 quote that excludes the pump, the permit, and the electrical.

## Rebates, tax credits, and financing

**Utility rebates:** Many utilities rebate Energy Star dehumidifiers — typically $25–75 for portables, occasionally more for whole-house units. Check your utility's rebate finder before buying; the paperwork usually just needs the model number and an Energy Star certificate. The AprilAire E-Series' Energy Star Most Efficient status helps here.

**Federal tax credits:** As of 2026, whole-house dehumidifiers sometimes qualify under energy-efficiency home improvement provisions when installed as part of a broader HVAC efficiency project — but standalone dehumidifier installs generally don't. Don't count on it; treat any credit as a bonus and confirm with a tax professional.

**Contractor financing:** HVAC contractors routinely offer 12-month same-as-cash or low-APR financing on installs over $1,500. If cash flow is the constraint, financing the install while buying the unit outright is often the cheapest structure — contractor unit markup plus financing interest is the expensive combination.

**The ROI case:** A $2,500 all-in install protecting against a single $4,000 mold remediation pays for itself the first time it prevents one. Add the AC-runtime savings (dry air feels cooler, so thermostats go up 1–2°F) and the protection of framing, flooring, and stored belongings, and the payback math is kind even before comfort enters it.

## Total cost of ownership

Installation is the big one-time cost, but keep the full picture: the unit ($1,000–$2,500), installation ($500–$1,500+), running costs ([~$15–$45/month in season](/posts/whole-house-dehumidifier-energy-cost-per-month/)), and near-zero maintenance (washable filters, annual drain check). Against the alternative — portable units replaced every 3–5 years, or mold remediation at $2,000–$6,000+ — the whole-house math usually wins within a few years.
