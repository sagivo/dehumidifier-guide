"Set it and forget it" works for a dehumidifier — until the first cold snap, when your windows start weeping and you discover the one setting that was perfect in August is a mold factory in January. Indoor humidity targets are seasonal, and the difference between 45% and 30% is the difference between comfort and condensation damage. Here's the full seasonal playbook: the EPA numbers, the winter temperature chart, and exactly where to set your humidistat.

## The master rule: 30–50%, shifting with the season

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's standing guidance is simple: keep indoor relative humidity **below 50% in warm weather**, with **30% as low as you should accept in cold weather** — a 30–50% band year-round. Within that band, though, the ideal point moves with the calendar:

| Season | Target indoor RH | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Summer (hot, humid) | **40–50%** | Below 50% starves mold and dust mites; 40–50% feels comfortable and keeps the AC efficient |
| Shoulder seasons | **40–45%** | Moderate temps, moderate outdoor humidity — the easy months |
| Winter (mild, 30°F+) | **35–40%** | Comfortable without stressing windows |
| Winter (cold, 10–20°F) | **30–35%** | Prevents condensation on glass and in wall cavities |
| Deep cold (~0°F and below) | **25–30%** | Cold surfaces condense moisture at levels that feel fine in milder weather |

The summer target is about **health and comfort**; the winter target is about **protecting the house**. Those are different problems, so don't expect one number to do both jobs.

## Why humidity goes wrong at the edges

Outside the 30–50% band, problems compound fast — and knowing them tells you which direction to adjust.

**Above 60%: the mold line.** Mold thrives when relative humidity exceeds 60%, especially in warm, stagnant spots — behind furniture against exterior walls, in closets, under sinks. Dust mites multiply above 50%. If you ever measure 60%+ in a living space, that's not a comfort issue, it's a maintenance emergency: find the moisture source, ventilate, and run dehumidification until you're solidly under 50%.

**Below 30%: the dry-air line.** Dry skin, chapped lips, bloody noses, static shocks with every doorknob, sore throats in the morning — and the house suffers too: wood floors gap, furniture joints crack, musical instruments detune. If you're under 30% in winter, the usual culprits are excessive ventilation or a heating system dragging in cold dry air; the fix is usually *less* air exchange, not more equipment.

**The condensation trap (winter only).** This is the sneaky one: 45% feels perfect and measures fine — but the inside surface of your windows is 15°F colder than the room. Moisture condenses on the glass, runs into the sill, and breeds mold in the window frame while your hygrometer insists everything is fine. Condensation on windows is the house telling you the humidity is too high *for that outdoor temperature*. Act on it: dry the area and drop the target.

## The winter chart: match humidity to outdoor temperature

The standard ASHRAE-derived guidance HVAC contractors use ties your indoor target directly to what's happening outside. Colder glass means a lower safe humidity:

| Outdoor temperature | Max recommended indoor RH |
|---|---|
| 30°F or warmer | 40% |
| 20°F | 35% |
| 10°F | 30% |
| 0°F | 25% |
| −10°F or colder | 20% |

Two caveats: first, these are *maximums* to prevent condensation, not comfort targets — in a well-insulated house with good windows you can often run a few points higher, and in a drafty house with single-pane glass you'll need to run lower. Second, your windows are the canary: **no condensation means your current level is fine regardless of the chart; morning condensation means drop 5 points and re-check.** One 30-second window check each morning in winter beats any chart.

Energy Star, the EPA, and the DOE all converge on the same advice: manage indoor humidity proactively, vent moisture-producing activities (showers, cooking, dryers) directly outdoors, and treat persistent condensation as a signal to act — it can damage window frames and surrounding walls and feed mold and mildew over time.

## Summer settings: where the dehumidifier earns its keep

Summer is dehumidifier season in most of the country, and the setpoint question comes up constantly. The answers:

- **Whole-house humidistat: 45%.** This is the industry-standard summer setpoint. It's 5 points below the EPA's 50% ceiling (a safety margin for sensor error and room-to-room variation), low enough to shut down dust mite reproduction, and high enough that the unit isn't running constantly trying to hit an unnecessarily dry target. Every degree of over-drying costs electricity — our [energy cost breakdown](/posts/whole-house-dehumidifier-energy-cost-per-month/) shows what in-season running actually costs.
- **Basements and crawl spaces: 45–50%, never above 60%.** These spaces run damper than the living room by nature. If your crawl space sits at 55% in August, that's acceptable; if it sits at 65%, it needs its own dedicated unit with its own humidistat — see our [crawl space guide](/posts/crawl-space-dehumidifier-guide/).
- **Bedrooms: 40–50%.** Slightly drier air sleeps better and keeps dust mites (the main bedroom allergen) suppressed.

**Don't set 35% in summer** thinking drier is better. Below ~40% in air-conditioned space you gain nothing — no additional mold protection, no comfort gain — and you pay for it in runtime and wear. The [how-to-choose guide](/posts/how-to-choose-whole-house-dehumidifier/) covers sizing so your unit hits 45% in normal cycles rather than running 24/7.

## Winter settings: mostly hands-off, with one adjustment

In most heated homes, winter humidity **falls on its own** — cold outdoor air holds little moisture, and heating it drops relative humidity dramatically. Many homes sit at 25–35% all winter with no dehumidifier running at all. So the winter playbook is mostly about *not* fighting physics:

1. **Turn the dehumidifier's summer setpoint off or raise it.** There's no point holding 45% when the house naturally sits at 32%. Set the humidistat to your winter target (35–40%) so it only runs during warm spells or in the damp basement.
2. **Watch the windows during the first cold snap.** That's when condensation appears. Drop the target 5 points at a time until the glass stays dry.
3. **Basements still need attention.** An unfinished basement can hold 60%+ in winter while the upstairs sits at 30%. Don't assume the whole house moves together — this is exactly why [basement-specific sizing](/posts/what-size-dehumidifier-for-1500-sq-ft-basement/) matters.
4. **If you're under 30% and uncomfortable**, that's a humidification problem, not a dehumidification one. Ventilate less, and consider a humidifier for the living space — your dehumidifier's job in January is mostly to stay out of the way.

## Measure before you adjust

All of these numbers are guesses until you measure. A digital hygrometer costs a few dollars — put one in the bedroom, one in the living room, and one in the basement or crawl space, and leave them 24 hours. You'll routinely find **10+ point spreads** between floors: 45% upstairs, 58% in the basement is a completely normal house, and it tells you exactly where dehumidification is needed.

Calibrate cheap hygrometers once a year with the salt test (a sealed bag with damp salt reads 75% RH — adjust your unit's offset accordingly). And measure in **several rooms**, not one: the EPA's own guidance emphasizes checking whether high humidity is localized (basement, attic) or house-wide, because the fix is different. Localized dampness wants a local unit; house-wide dampness is the case for a whole-house system tied into the ductwork — the [portable vs whole-house comparison](/posts/portable-vs-whole-house-dehumidifier/) walks through that decision, and the [capacity sizer](/tools/capacity-sizer/) sizes the answer.

## The seasonal calendar, summarized

- **April–May:** Fire up the dehumidifier, set 45%, check the filter after winter idle.
- **June–September:** Hold 40–50%. Check the basement/crawl space monthly — that's where trouble starts silently.
- **October:** Keep 45% until the heat runs steadily, then raise the setpoint to 40% and start watching windows.
- **November–March:** Target 30–40% guided by the outdoor-temperature chart. Condensation on glass = drop 5 points. Basement unit stays on its own 50% setpoint.
- **Year-round:** If any room reads above 60% or below 30% for more than a day or two, something needs fixing — a moisture source, a ventilation problem, or a unit that's the [wrong size for the space](/posts/dehumidifier-sizing-by-climate-zone/).

Get the seasonal rhythm right and the house stays in the healthy band all year: comfortable summers, condensation-free winters, and a dehumidifier that runs when it should and rests when it shouldn't.
