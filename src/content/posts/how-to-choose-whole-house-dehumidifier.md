A whole-house dehumidifier is a several-thousand-dollar decision that lives in your home for a decade. The good news: the whole decision collapses into five questions. Get these five right and the specific model almost picks itself. Get the wrong one wrong — especially sizing or drainage — and even a great unit will disappoint you.

## Decision 1: AHAM sizing — the only number that counts

Forget every "pint" number on a box until you know which test it came from. The **AHAM rating** is measured at 80°F and 60% relative humidity — a real-world summer condition. **Saturation numbers** are measured near 90°F / 90% RH, where the air holds far more moisture, so they run roughly **double** the AHAM figure. AlorAir's box says 120 pints on the HD55; it's a 55 AHAM unit. AprilAire and Santa Fe advertise AHAM numbers directly.

Our full decoder: [AHAM vs saturation pints explained](/posts/aham-vs-saturation-pints-explained/).

**Sizing cheat sheet (AHAM pints, humid climate):**

| Home size | Starting AHAM pints | Typical pick |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 1,500 sq ft | 30–55 | AlorAir HD55 (55 pt) |
| 1,500–2,500 sq ft | 55–70 | Santa Fe Compact70, AprilAire E070 |
| 2,500–4,000 sq ft | 70–90 | AprilAire E080, AlorAir HDi90 |
| 4,000–5,500 sq ft | 90–100 | AprilAire E100 Pro |
| 5,500–7,200 sq ft | 100–130 | AprilAire E130 |

That starting figure is ~10 AHAM pints per 500 sq ft. Then apply the rules: **add 20–30% headroom** (a unit running at 70% capacity lasts longer and handles heat waves), **size up** for damp basements, vented crawl spaces, or older leaky construction, and **size down slightly** for tight, encapsulated spaces. Climate matters enormously — a Gulf Coast home needs far more capacity than the same house in the Mountain West. Our [sizing-by-climate-zone guide](/posts/dehumidifier-sizing-by-climate-zone/) has the zone tables, and the [capacity sizer tool](/tools/capacity-sizer/) does the arithmetic.

## Decision 2: Drainage — plan it before you buy

This is the decision people forget and regret. A whole-house dehumidifier doesn't collect a cute little tank of water — it pulls **gallons per day**. A bucket is not a plan. You have two options:

- **Gravity drain:** The unit sits above a floor drain, sump pit, or standpipe, and water flows downhill. Simple, silent, nothing to break — but it requires the right geometry.
- **Condensate pump:** Pushes water uphill or across the house to a drain. Some units have one built in — the **AlorAir Sentinel HDi90** is the standout here for crawl spaces, where gravity options are usually nonexistent. Otherwise, an external pump adds cost and a part that can fail.

Walk your install location before shopping. Find the drain path, measure the rise, and let that determine whether you need a pump model. For crawl spaces specifically, read the [crawl space guide](/posts/crawl-space-dehumidifier-guide/).

## Decision 3: Ducted vs standalone

**Ducted** units tie into your HVAC ductwork, pulling humid air from the house and returning dry air through the ducts. Coverage is even, the unit can live in the attic or utility room out of the way, and your existing thermostat ecosystem stays in charge. The cost: professional installation is the norm (AprilAire's line is heavily pro-channel for this reason), and the install labor is a real line item.

**Standalone** units sit where the problem is — the basement, the crawl space, the utility room — plug into a standard outlet, and dehumidify the surrounding air. Simpler, cheaper to install, and often the right call when the moisture problem is localized to one zone. The limitation is range: a standalone unit in the basement does less for a humid second floor.

Rule of thumb: ducted for whole-home coverage, standalone for zone problems (basements, crawl spaces, additions).

## Decision 4: Climate and space type

Where you live and what space you're treating change the answer as much as square footage:

- **Gulf Coast / Southeast:** High ambient humidity year-round. Size at the top of every range; consider the E100 Pro or E130 rather than stretching a smaller unit.
- **Mid-Atlantic / Midwest:** Serious summers, dry-ish winters. A mid-range unit (E080, HDi90) sized with headroom handles it.
- **Arid regions:** You may only need dehumidification for a basement or crawl space. Don't buy whole-home capacity for a zone problem — the HD55 or Compact70 may be plenty.
- **Basements:** Cooler air holds less moisture but feels damper; below-grade seepage adds load. Size up one step.
- **Crawl spaces:** See the dedicated guide. Vented crawl spaces in humid climates are the hardest job on this list — they need dedicated units with pumps and usually encapsulation.

## Decision 5: Budget — what the tiers actually buy

| Tier | Price range | What you get | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value | ~$500–1,150 | Honest AHAM capacity at the low end, fewer frills, saturation marketing to decode | AlorAir HD55, HDi90 |
| Mid | ~$1,300–1,900 | Strong build, real filtration options, longer warranties | Santa Fe Compact70, AprilAire E080 |
| Premium | ~$2,000–2,600 | Top efficiency ratings, biggest capacities, pro-channel support | AprilAire E100 Pro, E130, Santa Fe Advance90 |

Money buys three things in this category: **honest capacity at high efficiency** (Energy Star Most Efficient units cost less to run for years), **warranty length** (5–6 years across this lineup), and **filtration** (Santa Fe's MERV-13, if indoor air quality matters to you). Don't buy capacity you can't drain and don't drain capacity you didn't size — the [operating cost tool](/tools/operating-cost/) will show you what each tier costs over five years.

## The 60-second version

1. Estimate AHAM pints: ~10 per 500 sq ft, add 20–30% headroom, adjust for climate and dampness.
2. Find your drain path; if there isn't one, you need a pump model like the HDi90.
3. Decide ducted (pro install, whole home) vs standalone (zone problem).
4. Match the unit to your climate zone and space type, not just square footage.
5. Pick the tier that fits, favoring efficiency and warranty within it.
