Sizing a crawl-space dehumidifier is where most buyers go wrong in both directions. Some buy a 50-pint unit for a 1,500 sq ft vented crawl and watch it run around the clock without ever hitting 55% humidity. Others chase the biggest number on the box — a 198-pint "saturation" rating that turns out to be barely 90 pints in the conditions of an actual crawl space. The truth is that sizing has a real method: pints per day against square footage, adjusted for encapsulation, ceiling height, and climate, and anchored to the one rating manufacturers don't put on the box.

This guide walks through that method with verified manufacturer numbers so you can buy once, size right, and never think about it again.

## How crawl-space sizing actually works

A dehumidifier's capacity is rated in pints of water removed per day (PPD). In a crawl space, that number has to cover the square footage, the moisture load from the ground and outdoor air, and — critically — the temperature the unit actually runs at. Crawl spaces are cool, often in the 50s and 60s year-round, and every dehumidifier removes less water at lower temperatures. A unit's headline number was almost certainly measured in a warm lab.

The baseline rule of thumb used by crawl-space pros: **about 10 pints per day for every 500 sq ft of sealed crawl space**, and roughly double that — 20 pints per 500 sq ft — in humid Gulf and Southeast climates where outdoor air is doing half the damage. Condition-based brackets that track those rules:

- **Up to ~1,000 sq ft, moderately damp:** 50–70 pints/day
- **1,000–1,500 sq ft, damp:** 70–90 pints/day
- **1,500–2,000 sq ft, very damp:** 90–120 pints/day
- **2,000+ sq ft or chronic moisture:** 120+ pints/day, or two units

Those brackets match what the manufacturers publish, once you read the fine print — which is the next section.

## Real manufacturer coverage ratings

Published square-foot ratings are the fastest way to sanity-check your bracket, and the three dominant brands publish them differently enough that you need to know whose rules you're reading.

**AprilAire E070** is rated 70 pints/day, and AprilAire's own FAQ names it their pick for crawl spaces "up to 2,800 sq ft." That's the generous end of the spectrum — it reflects a sealed, moderately damp crawl in an average climate.

**Santa Fe Compact70** (model 4044110) is also rated 70 pints/day at AHAM conditions, but Santa Fe publishes a range instead of an upper bound: **crawl-space sizing of 1,800–2,600 sq ft (9,000–13,000 cubic feet)**, and here's the fine print that matters — those numbers assume a **5-foot ceiling**. Crawl-space ceilings are low, and cubic footage is the real load variable: a 2,000 sq ft crawl with 6-foot ceilings holds 20% more air than the rating assumes. Santa Fe's basement ratings, by contrast, assume 10-foot ceilings. If your crawl is tall, round up.

Santa Fe's current Ultra lineup gives a clean ladder above the Compact70: the **Ultra70** (70 pints/day at 80°F/60%RH, up to 1,800 sq ft, ductable into HVAC) and the **UltraV100, UltraV125, and UltraV155** (100, 125, and 155 pints/day AHAM, rated up to 2,300, 3,000, and 3,500 sq ft respectively). Note that an older **Ultra120** (120 pints, up to 3,000 sq ft) still appears at some retailers, and a historical **Ultra98** shows up in legacy listings — neither is in Santa Fe's current catalog, so don't cite coverage figures for them from memory.

**AlorAir Sentinel HDi90** is rated **90 pints/day at AHAM conditions and 198 pints/day at saturation**, for crawl spaces up to 2,600 sq ft, with a built-in condensate pump that lifts 14.7 feet. Its bigger sibling, the **HDi120**, is rated 120 pints/day AHAM (235 at saturation) for up to 3,300 sq ft. That 90-to-198 gap is the perfect illustration of why the test basis matters — more on that below.

## The rating that matters: AHAM vs. saturation vs. DOE pints

Three test conditions, three very different numbers from the same machine:

- **AHAM pints** — tested at 80°F and 60% relative humidity. This is the standard, comparable basis across brands, and the number you should use to compare models against each other.
- **Saturation pints** — tested at 90°F and 90% RH. Near-maximum conditions that a crawl space essentially never sees. As a rule of thumb, a unit marketed at "90 pints" of saturation capacity may remove only ~50 pints at AHAM conditions. The HDi90's 198-pint saturation figure versus its 90-pint AHAM rating is exactly this gap in one machine.
- **DOE pints** — tested around 65°F and 60% RH (some listings cite 73°F/60%RH). This is closest to actual crawl-space conditions and, per multiple pro sources, the most honest sizing number. The drop from the headline is sobering: the Santa Fe Compact70's 70 AHAM pints fall to **43.7 pints at 65°F/60%RH** — a ~38% haircut. The HDi120's 120 AHAM pints become 93 at DOE conditions.

The practical advice from crawl-space contractors: **size off the DOE number and ignore the marketed big number.** A "70-pint" unit that delivers 44 pints in your 60°F crawl behaves like a 44-pint unit in every way that matters. This is the same lesson as the [AHAM vs. saturation pints explainer](/posts/aham-vs-saturation-pints-explained/), applied to the buying decision. [Climate zone matters too](/posts/dehumidifier-sizing-by-climate-zone/) — the DOE number still assumes moderate conditions, and a Gulf Coast crawl needs the doubling rule on top.

## Encapsulation, ceiling height, and moisture load: the three adjustments

Published ratings assume a friendly version of your crawl space. Adjust for the real one.

**Encapsulated vs. vented.** Encapsulation — sealed vents, a continuous vapor barrier on the floor and walls, sealed rim joists — is the single biggest variable. It cuts moisture load so dramatically that the same 1,000 sq ft crawl needs ~50–70 pints sealed and **90+ pints vented** — a one- to two-class step up. If you're on the fence about encapsulation, our [crawl-space encapsulation checklist](/posts/crawl-space-encapsulation-dehumidifier-checklist/) walks through the full job, because encapsulating first and sizing second is cheaper than sizing for a leaky crawl.

**Ceiling height.** Published crawl ratings assume short ceilings — Santa Fe's assume five feet. Measure your actual clearance and convert to cubic footage: a 1,500 sq ft crawl at 6 feet holds 9,000 cubic feet, which is what the Compact70's upper rating covers at 5 feet over 2,600 sq ft. Tall crawl, tall needs.

**Active moisture.** No dehumidifier out-dries standing water. If there's water intrusion — poor grading, failed gutters, a high water table — fix drainage first. A dehumidifier sized for normal moisture load will run forever against an active leak and fail early doing it. Domyown's spec sheets put it bluntly: the area must be free of water intrusion or excessive fresh-air infiltration for the rating to mean anything.

## Undersized vs. oversized: what actually goes wrong

**Undersized is the dangerous direction.** An undersized unit runs nonstop, never reaches the 50–55% RH target, and burns electricity doing it. This is the sizing mistake crawl-space pros warn about, and it's the one that costs real money — both in energy and in the mold remediation the underperformance allows. If your rule-of-thumb bracket says 70–90 and the unit you're eyeing is 50, don't talk yourself into the smaller machine.

**Slightly oversized is generally fine — and often recommended.** This is where dehumidifiers differ from air conditioners, and the confusion is common enough to address directly. An oversized AC is a classic problem: it cools the air quickly, satisfies the thermostat, and shuts off before it has run long enough to remove moisture — leaving a cold-but-clammy space. That mechanism doesn't transfer to dehumidifiers because they're controlled by a **humidistat**, not a thermostat. There's no temperature setpoint to satisfy early; the unit runs until the humidity target is actually reached, then rests. A slightly bigger unit just reaches 50% RH faster and runs fewer total hours.

The only caveat: a grossly oversized unit in a very small, tightly closed room can cycle on and off frequently. That's a noise-and-wear annoyance, not a failure to dehumidify. Given that the industry's own published ratings lean conservative and crawl spaces run colder than the ratings assume, most buyers are better served rounding up one class than rounding down. ## Putting it together: a worked example

Say you have a 1,400 sq ft crawl space in Tennessee, vented, with 5-foot ceilings and no standing water — moderately damp, not swampy.

1. **Baseline bracket:** 1,000–1,500 sq ft, damp → 70–90 pints/day.
2. **Encapsulation adjustment:** vented, not encapsulated → step up one to two classes → target 90–120 pints/day AHAM.
3. **Climate check:** Tennessee is humid but not Gulf Coast — no full doubling, but don't pick the low end either.
4. **DOE reality check:** if you pick a 90-pint AHAM unit, expect roughly 55–65 pints at real crawl-space temperatures. A 120-pint AHAM unit delivers closer to 90+ at DOE conditions — which is what the bracket actually asks for.
5. **Match to a real model:** the [AlorAir Sentinel HDi90](/dehumidifiers/alorair-sentinel-hdi90/) (90 AHAM / 198 saturation, up to 2,600 sq ft, built-in pump) sits at the low end of the adjusted bracket — viable if the crawl is on the drier side and [drainage is sorted](/posts/dehumidifier-drainage-options-gravity-vs-pump-vs-bucket/). The 120-pint class (e.g., the HDi120 at up to 3,300 sq ft) covers the adjusted bracket with headroom. The [AprilAire E070 Pro](/dehumidifiers/aprilaire-e070-pro/) (70 AHAM, up to 2,800 sq ft per AprilAire) would be the choice only if this crawl were sealed — which is the [encapsulation](/posts/crawl-space-encapsulation-dehumidifier-checklist/) argument in one sentence.

Buy the bracket, check the DOE number, and size for the crawl you have — not the crawl the brochure assumed.
