Crawl space encapsulation is the most complete answer to a damp crawl space: seal the whole environment against moisture instead of fighting it one leak at a time. But encapsulation has a failure mode that contractors see constantly — a beautifully sealed crawl space that still grows mold, because the project skipped the one component that keeps the sealed air itself dry.

The dehumidifier is the linchpin. The vapor barrier stops ground vapor, sealed vents stop humid outside air, the sump pump removes liquid water — but none of them remove moisture from the air trapped inside the sealed space. That takes an active dehumidifier running continuously. Here's the full checklist, in the order professionals do the work, plus what it costs and the mistakes that leave mold behind.

## Why the dehumidifier comes with the system, not after it

An encapsulated crawl space is a sealed box. Sealing stops new moisture from pouring in, but it also stops moisture from escaping. Construction moisture in the framing, minor air leaks around penetrations, condensation on cold ducts and pipes — all of it stays inside the box, and relative humidity climbs until something active removes it.

This is why every credible encapsulation checklist ends with a dehumidifier sized to the sealed space. The industry rule of thumb: 50–70 pints (AHAM) for a typical sealed crawl, 70–100 pints for humid regions or larger footprints. And always size from the **AHAM** rating — the honest 80°F/60% RH number — not the inflated saturation figure some brands market. (Our [AHAM vs. saturation pints explainer](/posts/aham-vs-saturation-pints-explained/) shows why the difference can be nearly 2x.)

For the models we track, that points at the [Santa Fe Compact70](/dehumidifiers/santa-fe-compact70/) (70 honest pints in a 12-inch-tall cabinet that hangs between floor joists) or the [AlorAir Sentinel HDi90](/dehumidifiers/alorair-sentinel-hdi90/) (90 honest pints with a built-in condensate pump, around $1,000–$1,150) for sealed spaces with no low drain to work with.

## The encapsulation + dehumidifier checklist

### 1. Deal with standing water first

A dehumidifier handles water vapor. It does not handle liquid water. If the crawl space collects standing water — after rain, from a high water table, from grading that slopes toward the foundation — fix that before anything else:

- Install an **interior perimeter drain** (drain tile) that routes water to a collection point.
- Install a **sump pit and pump** at that collection point. The sump handles the liquid; the dehumidifier handles what the sump can't.
- Fix exterior grading so water runs away from the foundation, and make sure gutters discharge well clear of the house.

Laying a vapor barrier over a puddle just hides the puddle. Water that can't be removed mechanically will defeat everything below it.

### 2. Clear, clean, and remediate

Strip the space down to a workable shell:

- Remove debris, stored junk, and old fallen insulation.
- Pull down old fiberglass insulation hanging between the floor joists — in an encapsulated crawl space, the insulation moves to the foundation walls and rim joist instead.
- Remediate any existing mold and treat or remove pest damage. Encapsulating over active mold seals the problem in, it doesn't solve it.

### 3. Lay the vapor barrier — floor and walls

The vapor barrier is the signature piece of the encapsulation, and the details matter more than the material cost:

- Use **reinforced polyethylene, 20-mil preferred** (12 or 16-mil minimum for a pro job). Building code only calls for 6-mil, but thicker poly is both less permeable and far more puncture-resistant — and this is a space people crawl through for service.
- Cover the **floor completely** and run the barrier **up the foundation walls to the rim joist**, wrapping piers and columns.
- Overlap seams by **12 inches** and seal every seam and edge with compatible tape or mastic. An unsealed seam is a moisture highway.
- Mechanically fasten the wall sections — don't rely on adhesive alone.

### 4. Seal the foundation vents

Every vent to the outside gets sealed shut. Open vents are the reason old vented crawl spaces stay humid: in summer, hot humid outside air enters, hits cool crawl-space surfaces, and condenses. Seal vents with foam board cut to fit, sealed at the edges, or with purpose-made vent covers. This step is what turns the crawl space from "vented" to "sealed" — and it's what makes the dehumidifier's job possible.

### 5. Seal every penetration

Pipes, ducts, electrical conduits, dryer vents, and cable runs all punch through the foundation or rim joist. Seal each one with compatible caulk, spray foam, or gaskets. Air sealing the rim joist and band board area is the highest-value part of this step — it's where the most air leakage happens in most houses. A vapor barrier with leaky penetrations is a bucket with holes.

### 6. Insulate the rim joist and walls

With the vents sealed, the foundation walls become part of the thermal boundary:

- Apply **rigid foam board (2-inch, ~R-10)** to the foundation walls and rim joist, sealing the edges with spray foam.
- This both insulates and forms an air barrier, preventing condensation on cold masonry and wood in winter.

### 7. Seal and insulate HVAC ducts in the crawl

Any ductwork running through the crawl space needs sealing with mastic and insulating — unsealed, uninsulated ducts in a sealed crawl space sweat, leak conditioned air, and give condensation a home. Some encapsulations also add a small **conditioned-air supply** from the HVAC system to the crawl space (a code-accepted alternative or supplement to a dehumidifier in some jurisdictions), but a dedicated dehumidifier gives you direct humidity control regardless of whether the HVAC is running.

### 8. Install the dehumidifier — sized, placed, and drained

Now the linchpin goes in, and it goes in last so it isn't fighting construction moisture for months:

- **Size it** to the sealed space: 50–70 AHAM pints for most jobs, 70–100 for humid regions or large footprints. Run the numbers with our [capacity sizer](/tools/capacity-sizer/) rather than guessing.
- **Place it** centrally with clear airflow — point the dry-air discharge toward the largest open area, not at a wall or door. In low-clearance crawls, the 12-inch-tall [Compact70](/dehumidifiers/santa-fe-compact70/) hangs between joists; in taller spaces, elevate the unit on blocks for both airflow and drainage elevation.
- **Drain it continuously.** The dehumidifier runs 24/7, so the drain must too. Gravity-drain to the sump basin when the geometry allows; use a [built-in pump unit](/posts/crawl-space-dehumidifier-with-pump/) like the HDi90 when it doesn't. Decide this before the unit goes in — see our [drainage options guide](/posts/dehumidifier-drainage-options-gravity-vs-pump-vs-bucket/).
- **Control it** with a humidistat set around 45–50% RH, and put a remote-reading hygrometer down there so you can verify without crawling in.

### 9. Monitor and maintain

Encapsulation is a system, and systems need checking:

- Verify RH stays **30–50%**, and never above 60%.
- Check the dehumidifier's filter on its schedule (washable MERV filters every few months in a sealed space).
- Glance at the sump pump, drain lines, and barrier seams seasonally — a torn barrier or a dead sump undoes the whole investment quietly.

## What encapsulation with a dehumidifier costs

For a 1,000–1,500 sq. ft. crawl space, plan on roughly:

- **Full professional encapsulation with dehumidifier: $8,000–$15,000.** This is the complete system — sump and drainage work, barrier, sealing, insulation, and the unit.
- **Encapsulation without the dehumidifier: $4,000–$8,000.** Cheaper, and a partial fix — the exact failure contractors warn about.
- **Dehumidifier hardware:** roughly $500–$1,150 for a crawl-space unit like the [AlorAir Sentinel HD55](/dehumidifiers/alorair-sentinel-hd55/) or HDi90, $1,300–$1,770 for pro-grade [AprilAire](/dehumidifiers/aprilaire-e070-pro/) or [Santa Fe](/dehumidifiers/santa-fe-compact70/) units.
- **DIY materials:** a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars in poly, tape, foam board, and sealant for an accessible, dry crawl — but sump work, mold remediation, and tight-quarter installs are where DIY savings evaporate.

Get quotes that itemize the dehumidifier, the sump/drainage work, and the barrier separately. If a quote lumps everything into one number, you can't tell whether the dehumidifier in it is a 50-pint pro unit or a no-name box with inflated specs.

## Mistakes that leave mold behind

- **Skipping the dehumidifier.** The classic. A sealed, un-dehumidified crawl space traps moisture instead of venting it — often worse than the vented original.
- **Thin or unsealed barrier.** 6-mil poly with taped-only seams tears, and unsealed seams leak vapor around the whole system.
- **Encapsulating over water or mold.** Standing water and active mold must be handled first, not sealed in.
- **Buying on saturation pints.** A "120-pint" budget unit that actually removes 55 AHAM pints will run flat-out in a sealed humid crawl and still lose ground. Size on AHAM.
- **No-name units with no service network.** If the compressor fails in year two and there are no parts, no warranty support, and no installer who'll touch it, the encapsulation's linchpin is gone. Stick with brands that have U.S. service and multi-year warranties.

An encapsulated crawl space with a properly sized, properly drained dehumidifier is a semi-conditioned part of the house — dry framing, no mold food, and measurably better air quality upstairs. Get the checklist in order, don't skip the last line item, and check the hygrometer twice a year.
