The sticker price of a whole-house dehumidifier is only half the story. The other half lands on your electric bill every month for the next decade — so before you buy, it's worth knowing exactly what "640 watts" means in dollars. The good news: the math is simple, the numbers are public, and most buyers overestimate the cost.

## The short answer

A whole-house dehumidifier typically adds **$15–$45 per month** to the electric bill during the humid season. A mid-size Energy Star unit running a normal duty cycle sits near the bottom of that range; an undersized unit grinding 24/7 in a Gulf Coast August sits at the top.

The single biggest lever isn't the model you pick — it's whether the unit is sized right. A properly sized dehumidifier reaches its humidity setpoint and cycles off. An undersized one never catches up and runs continuously, which is how a $1,500 unit quietly becomes a $70/month appliance. If you haven't sized yet, do that first with our [capacity sizer](/tools/capacity-sizer/) — it pays for itself in the first summer.

## The formula (30 seconds)

```
Monthly cost = (watts ÷ 1,000) × hours run per day × 30 × your $/kWh rate
```

Watts come from the spec sheet. Hours per day is your duty cycle — how long the compressor actually runs, not how long the unit is switched on. Your $/kWh rate is on your electric bill. The U.S. residential average was **18.34¢/kWh in June 2026** (EIA data), but rates vary enormously: the Pacific Northwest can be under 11¢, while parts of California and New England top 30¢. Always use your own rate for a real estimate — or try our [operating cost calculator](/tools/operating-cost/), which does this math for every model on this site.

Two things jump out. First, the DOE-rated annual figures tell the efficiency story cleanly: the E080 is rated 632 kWh/yr (~$112/yr), the E100 821 kWh/yr (~$145/yr), and the E130 926 kWh/yr (~$164/yr) — those are standardized lab estimates, not marketing. Second, the jump from 8 to 24 hours a day roughly triples the bill, which is why duty cycle matters more than the wattage number on the box.

In practice, a correctly sized whole-house unit in a moderately humid climate runs something like 6–12 equivalent full-load hours per day during peak season and far less in spring and fall. Very few homes see true 24/7 operation outside of an undersized unit or an unusually wet basement.

## Why efficiency (IEF) matters more than wattage

It's tempting to shop by watts — lower must be cheaper, right? Not exactly. What you pay for is pints of water removed, and the metric that captures that is the **Integrated Energy Factor (IEF)**: liters of water removed per kilowatt-hour.

Energy Star Most Efficient whole-house dehumidifiers, including the AprilAire E080, E100, and E130, are rated at **2.35 L/kWh**. Standard units typically land around 1.8–1.95 L/kWh. That means the efficient unit removes roughly 20–30% more moisture per dollar of electricity. A 700W efficient unit can easily cost less to run than a 550W inefficient one doing the same job — because it finishes the job faster and cycles off.

This is also why the [AHAM vs saturation pints](/posts/aham-vs-saturation-pints-explained/) distinction matters for running costs: if you size using inflated saturation numbers, you buy a unit that's secretly undersized, it runs constantly, and your "efficient" purchase becomes the most expensive unit on the bill.

## Whole-house vs portable: the per-pint math

A portable 50-pint unit draws roughly 500–700W. One of them looks cheap next to a 640W whole-house unit — until you need two or three portables to cover the same square footage, each with its own bucket or hose, each running its own compressor. At that point you're burning 1,000–2,000W to do what a single ducted unit does at 640W, with worse efficiency per pint.

The crossover is straightforward: one room or a small apartment, portable wins. A whole floor, a basement plus living space, or a full house — the ducted unit wins on energy, on maintenance, and usually within a few years on total cost. ## Five ways to cut the running cost

1. **Size it right.** The 20–30% headroom rule from our [sizing guide](/posts/dehumidifier-sizing-by-climate-zone/) isn't just about performance — headroom is what lets the unit cycle instead of running flat-out.
2. **Seal the space.** Every cubic foot of humid outdoor air leaking in is water your dehumidifier pays to remove. Sealing rim joists and crawl space vents cuts runtime more than any efficiency rating.
3. **Set 45–50% RH, not 35%.** Each 5 points lower on the humidistat measurably increases runtime. 45–50% prevents mold and dust mites; going lower buys little and costs a lot.
4. **Keep the filter clean.** A clogged filter chokes airflow, forcing longer runtimes for the same moisture removal. Rinse washable filters monthly in dusty installs.
5. **Use the AC as an ally.** Air conditioning already dehumidifies. In summer, letting the AC handle sensible cooling while the dehumidifier handles the latent load — rather than fighting each other with competing setpoints — keeps both efficient.

## The bottom line

Budget roughly **$25–$40/month during the humid season** for a properly sized Energy Star whole-house dehumidifier at average U.S. rates — less in mild climates, more where electricity is expensive or the unit is undersized. That's the price of protecting the house from mold, musty odors, and moisture damage to framing and finishes, which routinely cost thousands to remediate. ## What a full year actually costs

Nobody runs a dehumidifier at peak duty for twelve months. A realistic year in a humid climate looks more like this for a 640W unit at 18.34¢/kWh:

- **Jun–Aug (peak):** ~10 hrs/day → ~$35/month → ~$105
- **Apr–May, Sep–Oct (shoulder):** ~5 hrs/day → ~$18/month → ~$72
- **Nov–Mar (off season):** ~1 hr/day or off → ~$0–4/month → ~$10

**Realistic annual total: roughly $150–$200/year** for a mid-size Energy Star unit — which lines up well with the DOE's rated figures (632 kWh/yr ≈ $116/yr for the E080 at national-average rates, 821 kWh/yr ≈ $151/yr for the E100). In arid climates the annual number can be under $100; in the Gulf Coast with an undersized unit, it can exceed $400. The range is wide, but it's driven by climate and sizing, not by which brand you bought.

## Your rate matters more than the model

At 11¢/kWh (Pacific Northwest), that 640W unit at 8 hrs/day costs about **$17/month**. At 32¢/kWh (parts of California and New England), the identical usage costs **$49/month**. Same machine, triple the bill. Before comparing models on efficiency, check your actual rate — including time-of-use plans, which can make overnight dehumidification dramatically cheaper if your utility offers off-peak pricing. A unit with a good humidistat and scheduling effectively lets you buy moisture removal at the cheapest hours of the day.
