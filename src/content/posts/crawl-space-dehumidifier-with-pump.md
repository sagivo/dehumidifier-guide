A crawl space dehumidifier without a drainage plan is a very expensive paperweight. The unit will pull gallons of water out of the air every day — and then it needs somewhere to put it. In a basement you might have a floor drain or a laundry sink nearby. In a crawl space, you almost certainly have neither. That's what makes the pump question the first question, not an accessory decision.

## Why crawl spaces need pumps

Picture the typical installation: the dehumidifier sits on the crawl space floor or hangs from the joists, and the nearest place water can legally and practically go is a sump pit ten feet away, a discharge line running up and out through the foundation wall, or a sink drain on the floor above. Gravity only helps if the destination is downhill from the unit. In a crawl space — usually the lowest point on the property — it rarely is.

A condensate pump solves this with a small reservoir and a float switch. Water drains from the dehumidifier into the reservoir; when it fills, the pump kicks on and pushes the water through 3/8" vinyl or PVC tubing to wherever it needs to go, including 15+ feet of vertical lift. Then it shuts off. It's the same technology behind every air conditioner condensate pump in America — boring, reliable, and essential.

Skip the pump and your options are grim: a bucket you crawl under the house to empty (you won't), or a gravity hose run to nowhere. Size the unit right with our [crawl space guide](/posts/crawl-space-dehumidifier-guide/) — then solve drainage with equal seriousness.

## Built-in pump vs external pump

**Built-in pump** (factory-installed inside the dehumidifier):
- One box, one warranty, no extra parts to buy or wire
- Pump is matched to the unit's condensate output
- If the pump fails, the whole unit usually needs service
- Fewer models to choose from

**External condensate pump** (separate $150–500 unit):
- Works with any gravity-drain dehumidifier
- Replaceable independently if it fails
- Adds a second device, a second power draw, and more tubing
- Lets you pick the best dehumidifier regardless of pump options

Neither is universally better. The built-in route is cleaner and simpler; the external route gives you more unit choices and easier pump replacement. What matters is that you have *a* plan — the failure mode to avoid is the $1,500 dehumidifier with a garden hose coiled next to it going nowhere.

## Planning the drainage run

Before the unit arrives, map the water's path:

1. **Find the destination.** Sump pit is ideal. Otherwise: a laundry sink, a standpipe, or an exterior discharge point above grade. Check local code — some jurisdictions restrict where condensate may discharge.
2. **Measure the lift and run.** Pumps are rated for vertical lift (typically 15–20 ft) and total run length. Longer runs and higher lifts need a stronger pump — don't undersize this either.
3. **Route the tubing.** 3/8" ID vinyl or PVC, secured so it can't kink, with a check valve near the pump to prevent backflow. In unconditioned spaces, insulate or heat-trace the exterior portion so it can't freeze shut in winter.
4. **Add a safety switch.** Most quality pumps include an overflow safety switch that shuts the dehumidifier off if the reservoir overfills. Wire it — it's the flood insurance for a $30 part.
5. **Test before you seal up.** Pour water into the reservoir and watch a full pump cycle before you close the crawl space door. Discovering a kinked line in August is nobody's idea of fun.

If you're encapsulating the crawl space (sealed vents, vapor barrier on the ground, sealed rim joists), do the drainage planning as part of that project — running the discharge line before the vapor barrier goes down is dramatically easier than after.

## Pump maintenance: the part everyone forgets

A condensate pump is a mechanical device with a float, and floats get gunky. Once a year — ideally before the humid season — pour a cup of white vinegar or a dilute bleach solution through the reservoir to kill algae, confirm the float moves freely, and verify the discharge line is clear. Algae-clogged pumps are the #1 cause of "my dehumidifier stopped working" service calls that aren't actually dehumidifier problems.

Also keep the dehumidifier's own filter clean: a choked filter reduces airflow, which reduces water removal, which means the pump cycles differently than expected — and in edge cases can mask a real drainage problem. The whole system is only as reliable as its least-maintained part.

## What happens when the pump fails (and how to prevent it)

Pumps fail in exactly two ways: the float sticks (usually algae or sediment), or the motor dies (usually age). The symptom is the same — water backs up — but the consequences differ by setup:

- **With a safety switch wired in:** the pump's overflow switch cuts power to the dehumidifier when the reservoir overfills. Result: humidity creeps back up, you notice the musty smell returning, you check the crawl space, you find the problem. Annoying, not catastrophic.
- **Without a safety switch:** the reservoir overflows onto the vapor barrier or crawl space floor. In an encapsulated crawl space, that's water sitting exactly where you paid to keep it dry.

This is why the safety switch isn't optional, and why the annual vinegar flush matters more than any other maintenance task on the pump. A $150–500 pump protecting a $1,500 dehumidifier protecting a $5,000 encapsulation job is the cheapest insurance in the whole system. When a pump reaches 5–7 years old, replacing it proactively during the annual checkup is reasonable — it's a 20-minute swap versus an emergency crawl in August.

One more failure mode worth knowing: **frozen discharge lines.** If the pump discharges to the exterior in a cold climate, the line can freeze shut in winter. The pump runs against a blocked line, the reservoir overfills, and the safety switch (you wired it, right?) shuts everything down. Route exterior discharge with a downward slope, keep the run short, and in cold zones consider discharging to an interior drain instead.
