A dehumidifier is a compressor and a fan in a metal box, so yes — it makes noise. The good news is that whole-house units cheat: unlike a portable humming away in your living room, a whole-house dehumidifier lives in the basement, mechanical room, or crawl space, where a 50-decibel hum becomes a rumor. Here's how loud these units actually are, how they compare to each other, and the placement and installation choices that decide whether you ever hear yours.

## The headline number: 50–60 dB at the unit

Decibel ratings for whole-house dehumidifiers typically land between **50 and 60 dBA** measured near the unit. For context:

| Sound | Approx. level |
|---|---|
| Quiet suburb, moderate rainfall | ~50 dB |
| Normal conversation | ~60 dB |
| Dishwasher mid-cycle | ~55–60 dB |
| Refrigerator hum | ~40–45 dB |
| Window air conditioner | ~55–65 dB |

So a whole-house dehumidifier is roughly conversation-level at the cabinet — louder than a fridge, quieter than most window ACs. The compressor cycling on and off is usually more noticeable than the fan drone, the same way you notice a fridge kicking on more than its steady hum.

Critically, that's measured **at the unit**. One HVAC-focused review of whole-house units put it well: most owners can't hear the unit from upstairs at all — it runs behind a closed door in a basement or mechanical room, so in the living areas it's effectively silent. That placement is the entire reason noise is a non-issue for most whole-house buyers, and it's the main comfort advantage over a stack of [portables humming in bedrooms and living rooms](/posts/portable-vs-whole-house-dehumidifier/).

## Model-by-model noise comparison

Manufacturers are inconsistent about publishing noise figures — several AprilAire spec sheets don't list dB at all — so we've compiled what the brands and spec sheets actually state for the units in our lineup:

| Model | Rated noise | Capacity (AHAM) | Typical location |
|---|---|---|---|
| AlorAir Sentinel HD55 | **<52 dBA** | 55 pints | Crawl space / basement |
| AprilAire E070 Pro | **53 dBA (ducted)** | 70 pints | Crawl space / basement |
| Santa Fe Compact70 | **55 dBA** | 70 pints | Crawl space / basement |
| AprilAire E100 Pro | ~55 dB (per review tables) | 100 pints | Basement / mechanical room |
| AlorAir Sentinel HD90 | **58 dBA** | 90 pints | Crawl space / commercial |
| AlorAir Sentinel HDi90 | **<58 dBA** | 90 pints | Crawl space / commercial |
| Santa Fe Advance90 | **59 dBA** | 90 pints | Basement / mechanical room |
| AprilAire E130 | ~53 dB (per review tables) | 130 pints | Mechanical room / large home |

A few things to read into this table carefully:

**A 7 dB spread is a big real-world difference.** The decibel scale is logarithmic: every 10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud to human ears. The 59 dBA Santa Fe Advance90 sounds meaningfully louder than the <52 dBA AlorAir HD55 — roughly 1.6x the perceived loudness. But capacity and cabinet size track with noise, so this is mostly the physics of moving more air and refrigerant through a bigger machine, not a quality gap.

**Manufacturer ratings aren't standardized.** Some brands measure in free air, some ducted, some at a stated distance, and some don't publish a figure at all (notice the "per review tables" caveats above — those come from third-party comparison tables, not the brand's spec sheet). Compare published numbers as directional, not as lab-grade. This is the same reason we insist on [comparing AHAM pints, not marketed saturation pints](/posts/aham-vs-saturation-pints-explained/) — when the measurement standard varies, only same-condition numbers are honest.

**Ducted ratings are the ones that matter.** AprilAire's 53 dBA figure for the E070 is explicitly a *ducted* rating — the number you'll actually experience once the unit is connected to ductwork. A ducted install attenuates fan noise substantially before it reaches any room, which is why ducted installs consistently get the "I never hear it" reviews.

## Ducted vs freestanding: where noise actually goes

The single biggest noise decision isn't which model you buy — it's how it's installed.

**Ducted installation (dedicated return to supply)** places the unit in an unfinished basement, attic, crawl space, or mechanical closet, and connects it to the HVAC ductwork. What you hear upstairs: the faint whoosh of air moving through ducts — roughly what your furnace already produces. The compressor, the fan motor, and the cabinet rumble never reach you directly. This is the standard install our [choosing guide](/posts/how-to-choose-whole-house-dehumidifier/) recommends, and noise is one of the reasons.

**Freestanding installation** puts the unit directly in the space it dehumidifies — a finished basement, a utility closet off the living room, an enclosed crawl space with a thin access panel. Now that 50–60 dB is in the room with you. It's still not *loud*, but it's a constant presence: noticeable on the phone, noticeable during a movie, noticeable in a home office.

**Practical rule:** if the unit must live freestanding in or near finished space, buy down the noise table — an [AlorAir HD55](/dehumidifiers/alorair-sentinel-hd55/) at <52 dBA is a much better neighbor than a 59 dBA unit. If you're ducting from a remote spot, any of these units disappears acoustically, so buy on capacity and efficiency instead.

## The four noise sources (and which ones you can fix)

Owners who do hear their unit are usually hearing one of these — and three of the four are installation details, not product defects.

### 1. Compressor and fan noise (the baseline)
This is the number on the spec sheet. You can't reduce it below the unit's rating, but you can *locate* it: a basement ceiling and a closed door take 50 dB down to near-nothing in the room above. Every finished-space noise complaint is really a location complaint.

### 2. Vibration transfer (the fixable one)
The most common "my dehumidifier is loud" cause is the unit's cabinet touching something that acts as a soundboard: ductwork, floor joists, a wall stud, a concrete pad it was slid against during install. Fix: rubber or cork isolation pads under the unit, flexible duct connectors instead of rigid ones where practical, and a finger-width of clearance between the cabinet and any framing. Our [installation cost guide](/posts/whole-house-dehumidifier-installation-cost/) notes that vibration isolation is part of what the labor line buys — it's worth confirming your contractor includes it.

### 3. Air-velocity noise in ducts
Undersized or kinked duct runs whistle. If the unit itself is quiet but you hear rushing air at the grilles, the ductwork is the culprit — a return grille placed in a bedroom closet (bad) vs. a hallway (good) changes the experience more than 3 dB of unit spec.

### 4. Condensate pump cycling (the surprise one)
The pump kicks on for a few seconds when its reservoir fills — a buzz and a click. In a quiet house at night, a pump bolted to a stud wall is noticeable. Fix: set the pump on a rubber pad, don't hard-mount it to framing, and if you have a [gravity-drain option](/posts/crawl-space-dehumidifier-with-pump/), use it — gravity drainage makes zero noise.

## Maintenance noise: the creeping kind

Units get louder over time for boring, preventable reasons:

- **Dirty filter.** A clogged washable filter forces the fan to work harder and raises pitch. Wash or vacuum the filter on the schedule in the manual — this fixes more "it got louder" complaints than anything else.
- **Loose panels.** Access panels and drain hose clamps vibrate loose over seasons. A five-minute annual check (panel screws, hose fittings, pump seating) keeps the install quiet.
- **Icing.** In cold spaces, coils that ice up and then defrost make clunks and drips. Units rated for cold spaces handle this, but if you're hearing new cyclic banging in a crawl space in winter, icing is a prime suspect.

## Buying for quiet: the checklist

1. **Check the dB rating before you buy.** If the brand doesn't publish one, treat that as information — brands that measure well tend to advertise it.
2. **Match the unit to the location, not the other way around.** Remote ducted spot? Any unit on the table is fine. Finished basement or closet near living space? Stay under ~55 dBA.
3. **Plan the install, not just the unit.** Isolation pads, flexible connectors, grille placement in hallways not bedrooms, and a gravity drain wherever possible — our [installation guide](/posts/whole-house-dehumidifier-installation-cost/) has the full scope.
4. **Budget for ducted if quiet matters.** A freestanding 70-pint unit in a finished basement will be a roommate; the same unit ducted from an unfinished corner will be a rumor. The [best whole-house picks](/posts/best-whole-house-dehumidifiers-2026/) and our [capacity sizer](/tools/capacity-sizer/) cover the rest of the buying decision.

The bottom line: whole-house dehumidifier noise is a solved problem for almost every buyer. The units aren't whisper-quiet at the cabinet, but their whole design — sitting in the mechanical spaces, ducted into the air system — means you buy the silence by default. Choose the install right, and the loudest thing you'll ever notice is the condensate pump.
