A dehumidifier's capacity is only real if the water has somewhere to go. A 90-pint unit with a full bucket or a kinked drain hose is, for all practical purposes, a very expensive paperweight for part of the day — every hour it spends switched off or backing up is moisture your house isn't shedding.

Whole-house and crawl-space dehumidifiers are designed for continuous drainage rather than bucket emptying, but there are three ways to get the water out of the unit: a gravity drain, a condensate pump, or a collection bucket. Each fits a different install, and picking the wrong one is one of the most common reasons an otherwise good dehumidifier underperforms. Here's how each works, what the installation actually requires, and how to match the method to your basement or crawl space.

## Method 1: Gravity drain — simplest and most reliable

A gravity drain relies on nothing but elevation. You connect a hose to the dehumidifier's drain port — usually a standard 3/4-inch garden-hose thread — and run it downhill to a floor drain, sump basin, laundry sink, or exterior penetration. Water flows out on its own, continuously, with zero moving parts.

This is why contractors love gravity drains: there's nothing to break. No pump to burn out, no float switch to stick, no electronics to fail at 2 a.m. Nearly every pro-grade whole-house unit in our lineup drains by gravity by default — the [AprilAire E070 Pro](/dehumidifiers/aprilaire-e070-pro/) ships with a 10-foot drain hose in the box, the [Santa Fe Compact70](/dehumidifiers/santa-fe-compact70/) includes an 8-foot hose, and the [AprilAire E100 Pro](/dehumidifiers/aprilaire-e100-pro/) uses a plain gravity line even though it's a 100-pint whole-house machine.

But gravity has one hard requirement: **the drain point must sit below the dehumidifier's drain port**, and the hose must hold a continuous downward slope for its entire run. No sags, no flat stretches, no uphill sections. A hose that dips and rises again will trap water, back up, and either trigger the unit's float shutoff or spill. That slope requirement is why so many crawl-space installs start with the unit [elevated on cinder blocks](/posts/crawl-space-dehumidifier-with-pump/) — a few inches of elevation buys a usable slope to the sump basin or drain.

Gravity drain is the right call when:

- There's a floor drain, sump basin, or sink already below the unit's position.
- You want the fewest failure points in a sealed crawl space you rarely visit.
- The installation is remote enough that a pump failure could go unnoticed for weeks.

Its limits are pure geometry. If the only drain is a laundry sink up at counter height, or the only way out is a foundation penetration above the unit, gravity can't do the job. That's when you bring in a pump.

## Method 2: Condensate pump — when the water has to go up

A condensate pump actively lifts collected water through a narrow discharge tube to a drain that's higher than the unit — a laundry sink, the rim of a sump basin, an overhead waste line, or an exterior discharge point. The pump sits either built into the dehumidifier or as a separate external unit beside it.

**Built-in pumps** are the cleanest option. The [AlorAir Sentinel HDi90](/dehumidifiers/alorair-sentinel-hdi90/) is our favorite example: a 90-pint (AHAM) crawl-space unit with the condensate pump integrated into the cabinet for around $1,000–$1,150. One less piece of equipment, one less plug, one less failure point — the pump runs on the unit's own controls. For sealed crawl spaces where you want to minimize service visits, a built-in pump is the default recommendation, which is why the HDi90 is also our best crawl-space pick overall (see our [crawl-space dehumidifier with pump](/posts/crawl-space-dehumidifier-with-pump/) guide).

**External condensate pumps** do the same job as a standalone box next to the unit. Crawl-space contractors commonly use them — professional installs often pair an AprilAire unit with a dedicated condensate pump and route the discharge through a wall or down to a gravel dry well below grade. An external pump is also the retrofit answer when you already own a gravity-drain unit and discover the drain geometry doesn't work: add the pump, keep the unit.

What to know about pumps:

- **Lift limits matter.** Integrated dehumidifier pumps typically lift about 15–16 feet vertically. Measure the actual rise from the unit to your drain point and confirm the pump's rating covers it before you commit.
- **They need power and maintenance.** A pump is a mechanical part in a damp environment; reservoirs can grow algae and float switches can stick. Plan on checking and cleaning the pump reservoir periodically — the same rhythm as your [filter maintenance](/posts/whole-house-dehumidifier-noise-levels/).
- **Secure the discharge end.** If you're draining into a sump basin, fasten the hose so the tip stays above the water line. A loose hose that slips below the surface can siphon sump water backward into the dehumidifier.
- **Cold climates need thought.** A discharge line running outside can freeze and block. Bury it below the frost line or route it to an interior drain if winters are severe.

## Method 3: Bucket — the portable compromise

The third option isn't really an option for whole-house equipment, but it deserves an honest accounting because it's how most people start. A portable dehumidifier collects water in an internal tank — typically 1–2 gallons — and shuts itself off with a full-bucket indicator when the tank is full. You carry the bucket to a sink, empty it, and restart the unit.

In humid weather, that can mean emptying once or twice a day, and every hour the unit sits idle with a full bucket is an hour it isn't dehumidifying. For a vacation home you visit monthly, a bucket unit is effectively off most of the time. For a crawl space you never enter, it's off permanently after the first fill.

Whole-house and pro-grade units are designed around this exact weakness: they're plumbed for continuous drainage so they run unattended for years. If you're comparing a [stack of portables against one whole-house unit](/posts/portable-vs-whole-house-dehumidifier/), the drainage story is half the argument — portables stop themselves, whole-house units don't. The bucket exists in this guide as the cautionary tale: if the plan involves anyone carrying water, the install isn't finished.

## Where should the water go?

The four practical destinations, in order of contractor preference:

1. **Sump basin.** The natural partner — water that would have collected in the basin gets there through the dehumidifier instead. Keep the hose end above the water line.
2. **Floor drain.** The easiest gravity option in an unfinished basement or mechanical room. Check local code — some municipalities restrict what can go down a floor drain tied to the sanitary sewer.
3. **Laundry or utility sink.** Common in basements, but usually at counter height, which means a pump. Secure the hose to the sink so it can't walk out onto the floor.
4. **Exterior discharge.** Through a foundation penetration to a dry well, daylight drain, or yard. Works with gravity or a pump, but watch for freezing in winter.

Whatever the destination, use a hose that can't kink, keep the run as short and straight as geometry allows, and make sure the end of the line can't be blocked — mulch, soil, and ice are the usual suspects outdoors.

## The decision: which drainage method for your install

| Your situation | Best method |
|---|---|
| Floor drain or sump basin below the unit | **Gravity drain** — simplest, nothing to fail |
| Only drain is a laundry sink or elevated line | **Condensate pump** (built-in like the HDi90, or external) |
| Sealed crawl space you rarely enter | **Built-in pump** or **gravity** — avoid buckets entirely |
| Retrofit: unit already installed, no low drain | **External condensate pump** — add it, keep the unit |
| Budget priority | **Gravity** — no pump to buy, maintain, or replace |

Two planning rules worth their weight:

- **Decide drainage before you buy.** A pump-equipped unit costs more up front, but an external pump added later costs more than the built-in version would have, and re-routing hoses through a finished basement costs more than either. This is also a line item in [whole-house dehumidifier installation cost](/posts/whole-house-dehumidifier-installation-cost/) — drainage routing is part of what the installer is charging for.
- **Gravity first, pump second.** If the geometry allows a gravity drain, take it. Every mechanical part you add to a sealed, rarely-visited space is a future service call waiting to happen. But don't force gravity where it can't work — a pump that's maintained beats a gravity line with a sag in it every time.
